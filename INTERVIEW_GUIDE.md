# Interview guide - model-risk-monitoring

## What it does, in three sentences

It takes the credit scorecard from my credit-risk-scorecard repo, rebuilds it as code, and then checks it the way a
bank's model-risk team would check a live model: is the population being scored still the one it was built on (PSI /
CSI), does it still rank risk (KS / Gini), and are its predicted default rates still right (calibration)? Because the
public data has no dates, it simulates six time windows with a documented, gradually increasing shift toward
high-utilisation, younger, riskier applicants. It writes an HTML validation report with red/amber/green flags and
auto-generated findings, plus a markdown report in the voice of a first-year analyst.

## Why each key decision was made (plain language)

- **Simulated windows, clearly labelled.** Give Me Some Credit is one snapshot. Rather than pretend, the config
  states exactly how each window is tilted, and the report says so. The metrics are real; the trend is designed.
- **The development window is drawn first and at random.** My first version let all windows compete for rows and
  it drained bad outcomes out of the development sample (bad rate fell from 6.7 % to 3.5 %). A scorecard built on a
  distorted sample makes every later check meaningless. Good lesson; it's in the README.
- **PSI on the score, CSI on the inputs.** PSI answers "did the population move?" CSI answers "which characteristic
  moved it?" - here utilisation, CSI 0.40 in Q5, which matches the injected story.
- **Stability and performance are reported separately.** The population went red on PSI (0.356) while KS stayed
  at 0.518 and calibration stayed within 0.5 pp. That combination means "review your cut-offs", not "rebuild
  the model". Conflating the two is the most common misread of a monitoring pack.
- **Absolute calibration gap instead of Hosmer-Lemeshow.** With 10,000+ rows per window the H-L test rejects tiny
  gaps that nobody would act on. A 2 pp gap is something pricing can act on.
- **A challenger as a yardstick.** Gradient boosting gains about 3 Gini points but is not reason-code explainable
  and decayed faster under the same drift. The report says "not a replacement candidate" and explains why.
- **Findings are generated by code.** The findings list in the report is produced from the thresholds, so the
  document can't drift from the numbers.

## What I'd do differently

- Use real booking cohorts with a 24-month performance window, and show how PSI leads KS/Gini by two years.
- Calibrate the RAG thresholds to portfolio economics (how much does a 0.25 PSI cost in approval-rate error?).
- Add override analysis, reject inference and fair-lending checks - a real validation is incomplete without them.
- Tune the challenger properly and validate it on an out-of-time window before quoting its lift.

## Five questions an interviewer would ask, with honest answers

**1. PSI is red in Q5. Why don't you recommend replacing the model?**
Because discrimination and calibration are both fine: KS 0.518 vs 0.555 at development, Gini down 7.1 %,
and actual minus predicted default rate +0.48 pp. The model is correctly saying that a riskier intake is
riskier. The business problem is that the same score cut-off now approves a riskier book, so the action is a cut-off
and approval-rate review, plus finding out why the intake changed.

**2. What is PSI, in one sentence, and where do 0.10 and 0.25 come from?**
PSI sums, over score deciles of the development sample, (actual share - expected share) x ln(actual / expected); it
is zero when the two distributions match. The 0.10 / 0.25 bands are long-standing industry rules of thumb, not
statistical thresholds - I say that in the report and list it as something a senior reviewer should challenge.

**3. Isn't simulating drift cheating?**
It would be if I hid it. The alternative was a dataset with no time dimension and nothing to monitor. Every tilt is in
config.yaml, the report quotes it, and the README explains that the trend is by construction. What the project shows is
the *machinery*: the metrics, the thresholds, how findings are written and what actions follow.

**4. What does WOE do and why smooth it?**
Weight of evidence replaces each bin of a characteristic with ln(share of goods / share of bads) in that bin, so the
logistic regression works on a risk-ordered, missing-value-safe transformation. I add 0.5 to each bin's good and bad
counts so an empty bin in a monitoring window does not produce an infinite WOE - and I flag that choice for review.

**5. What would SR 11-7 expect that this repo doesn't have?**
Independent review (I built and validated my own model), documented model use and limitations signed off by an
owner, override and exception tracking, fair-lending analysis, and a change log. The repo covers conceptual
soundness, ongoing monitoring and outcomes analysis, which are the three pillars, but a real validation is a
governance process, not a script.
